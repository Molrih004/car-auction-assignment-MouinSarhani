# Stakeholder Interviews

These transcripts were recorded during a scoping session held at VeloVault's Amsterdam office. Each interview lasted approximately thirty minutes. Names and job titles are used with permission.

The five people interviewed have different roles, different levels of experience with technology, and different reasons to care about the platform. They do not always agree with each other. Pay attention to what people do, what they want, and what worries them, not only to what they say the system should do.

**Stakeholder overview**

| # | Name | Role | Relationship to the platform |
|---|------|------|------------------------------|
| 1 | Marcus de Bruin | Founder & Chief Executive Officer | Owns the business, oversees from a distance |
| 2 | Diane Hoekstra | Private seller | Lists cars and sets up auctions |
| 3 | Tom Verhoeven | Collector / buyer | Browses and bids |
| 4 | Sandra Meijer | Operations Manager | Oversees and intervenes daily |
| 5 | Erik Smits | Head of IT | Responsible for the technical platform |

## Interview 1 — Marcus de Bruin, Chief Executive Officer

| | |
|---|---|
| **Role** | Founder and CEO |
| **Age** | 54 |
| **Interview context** | His office overlooking the showroom floor. Relaxed, talks in big-picture terms. His phone buzzed throughout. |

**Interviewer:** Marcus, thank you for making time. Can you tell me a bit about what VeloVault does and what you're hoping this platform will achieve?

**Marcus:** Of course. We've been in the classic car business for over a decade. I started out restoring Alfa Romeos in a rented garage in Haarlem, and VeloVault grew out of that twelve years ago. What makes us different from a generic auction site is the curation — we only deal in cars that are actually interesting. A 1967 Alfa Romeo Giulia, a 1973 Porsche 911 T, that sort of thing. Our buyers are collectors, not dealers looking to flip something for fifty euros profit.

The problem is that our old process — email, spreadsheets, newsletters — doesn't scale. We had one member of staff spending three days a week just coordinating bids. That's not a business, that's a hobby.

**Interviewer:** What did the old process look like from the customer's side?

**Marcus:** Slow, and a bit of a black box. A buyer replies to the newsletter with a bid and then hears nothing until the auction closes. Are they winning? Did anyone else bid? Did we even receive the email? Half of Sandra's phone calls were people asking "did you get my bid?" The good side of it was the personal touch. People know us by name here. I don't want to lose that feeling completely.

**Interviewer:** And the platform is meant to replace all of that?

**Marcus:** Mostly, yes. I want sellers to be able to come to the platform, register, add their car, set up an auction, and let buyers find it and bid on it. Without anyone at VeloVault having to touch it.

**Interviewer:** Who are the different types of users you're imagining?

**Marcus:** There are three, really. You have the sellers — they own the cars and want to sell them. You have the buyers — collectors, enthusiasts, people who want to bid. And then there's our own staff, who need to be able to oversee everything, intervene if something goes wrong, maybe remove a listing that turns out to be fraudulent.

**Interviewer:** Can you describe a typical buyer?

**Marcus:** Mostly men, if I'm honest, between forty and seventy. Plenty of successful professionals: a surgeon, a notary, a man who owns three bakeries. Some are very comfortable online, others still print the newsletter and circle cars with a pen. About a third of our buyers are in Belgium, some of them French-speaking, although everyone we deal with manages fine in Dutch or English. And lately we see younger buyers, early thirties, who found us through Instagram. They expect everything to work like the apps on their phone.

**Interviewer:** And sellers?

**Marcus:** More varied. Some are collectors thinning out their garage and know exactly what they're doing. Others inherited a car from a parent and have no idea what it's worth or how an auction works. That second group needs more guidance. Selling your late father's Mercedes is not a purely financial decision.

**Interviewer:** Coming back to the third group you mentioned. So your staff would have a different level of access than regular users?

**Marcus:** They'd have to, wouldn't they? I don't want a buyer being able to delete someone else's car listing. And I don't want a seller messing with another seller's auction.

**Interviewer:** You mentioned curation earlier. How does that work once sellers list their own cars?

**Marcus:** [laughs] Good question. I haven't fully figured that out. Today every car passes through us, so we know it's worth listing. With self-service, anyone can put anything online. I'd like our staff to keep an eye on things, but I don't want them to become the bottleneck again either.

**Interviewer:** Let's move from what the platform does to how it comes across. How should it feel to the people using it?

**Marcus:** Like walking into our showroom. Calm, a bit of polish, and the cars are the stars. Not flashing banners and "ONLY 2 HOURS LEFT!!!" in red capitals. Our customers spend fifty, eighty thousand euros on a car. They need to trust us before they place a bid. If it looks like a cheap auction site, they'll assume it is one.

**Interviewer:** Will you use the platform yourself?

**Marcus:** On my phone, mostly, to see how things are going. I run the whole business from my phone and my iPad, to be honest. You'll rarely find me behind a desk. So: which auctions are running, which ones end soon, whether that Giulia reached its reserve. I'm not going to be the one fixing things. That's Sandra.

**Interviewer:** What does success look like to you in a year's time?

**Marcus:** Honestly? I want to go to sleep knowing the platform is running without me worrying about it. If we have an auction ending at two in the morning and the system goes down, that's a disaster — we lose the seller's trust, we lose the buyer's trust, and word travels fast in this community. So reliability is not a nice-to-have. It's fundamental.

**Interviewer:** How many people do you expect to use it?

**Marcus:** Our newsletter goes out to about three thousand people. Maybe four hundred of them have ever placed a bid. We run around fifty auctions a month now. If this works, I'd like to double that within a year. But we will never be a mass-market website. It's a niche, and everybody in it knows everybody.

**Interviewer:** Are there any other concerns you'd flag at this stage?

**Marcus:** Security. Our users are giving us their email addresses and passwords. Some of them are quite private people. Some buyers don't want anyone to know they own a particular car, for insurance reasons or simply because they don't like attention. I need to know that data is handled properly. And I want to make sure that if someone registers as a buyer, they can't suddenly start doing things they're not supposed to do.

## Interview 2 — Diane Hoekstra, Classic Car Seller

| | |
|---|---|
| **Role** | Private seller |
| **Age** | 63 |
| **Interview context** | Came to the office with a folder full of paperwork about her cars. Precise and detail-oriented, occasionally corrected the interviewer on car history. |

**Interviewer:** Diane, you've sold two cars through VeloVault before, is that right?

**Diane:** Three, actually. A Citroën DS, a Lancia Fulvia, and last year a really beautiful Fiat 124 Spider. All through the old process, which honestly was fine when it was just a few emails back and forth, but I heard they're building something new so here I am.

**Interviewer:** Why are you selling?

**Diane:** Space, mostly. And age, if I'm honest. I've been collecting for thirty years, ever since my first job as an architect, and now that I'm retired the garage in Deventer is full and my knees don't enjoy crawling under cars anymore. I'm reducing the collection to the two I'll never part with. Each car I sell, I want it to go to someone who'll look after it. The price matters, of course, but that matters too.

**Interviewer:** What would you want the new process to look like?

**Diane:** I want to be able to log in, add my car, fill in all the details, and then set up an auction myself. Right now I have to email photos and descriptions to the VeloVault team and wait for them to put it in the system. That can take two or three days. And then I still have to check what they put online, because more than once something was wrong. They listed the Lancia as a 1971 instead of a 1969. For a collector that's not a detail, that's a different car.

**Interviewer:** What kind of details do you fill in about a car?

**Diane:** The basics — make, model, year, colour. Then the condition, which matters a lot for classics. I'd say excellent, good, fair, something like that. Mileage. And then a free-text description where I can write about the history of the car, any restoration work, that kind of thing. Oh, and I usually have an idea of what it's worth, so some kind of estimated value field would be useful.

The condition is tricky, though. Everyone thinks their own car is in excellent condition. My "good" might be somebody else's "fair". It would help if the platform explained what each option actually means.

**Interviewer:** How do you usually prepare that information?

**Diane:** I write the description at home first, in Word, on my laptop, in the evening when I have time. I'm fine with a computer. Email, online banking, all of that. Don't ask me about Facebook or Instagram, though, I've never bothered. Some of my descriptions are a full page: restoration history, previous owners, which invoices I still have. Then I'd copy it in. Long forms on a screen do tire me, I have to say. I'm forever pushing my reading glasses up and zooming in. The worst thing that can happen is that I spend an hour on a form and then it times out or I press the wrong button and everything is gone. That happened to me once on a government website and I nearly threw my laptop out of the window.

And photos, of course. Nobody buys a classic car without photos. Fifty of them, at least.

**Interviewer:** And for the auction itself?

**Diane:** I want to set a starting price — the minimum bid I'll accept to get things going. And a reserve price that I don't have to make public. If bidding doesn't reach that reserve, the car doesn't sell. That protects me. I also want to set the start and end date myself. Some sellers like a short, sharp auction — three days, creates urgency. I usually prefer ten days so more people have a chance to see it.

Although I must admit, the first time I sold through VeloVault I didn't understand the difference between the starting price and the reserve. I set them to the same amount. Sandra had to phone me to explain. So perhaps the screen should explain it.

**Interviewer:** Once the auction is running, what do you do?

**Diane:** Check it. Far too often. [laughs] Every morning with my coffee and again in the evening. I want to see the current bid, how many bids there are, and whether my reserve has been reached. Buyers don't need to know my reserve, but I want to know whether I'm there yet. With the old process I heard nothing until the end, and ten days feels very long when it's your car.

**Interviewer:** Has anything gone wrong in the past that you'd want the new system to prevent?

**Diane:** Yes, actually. Last year I listed the Fiat and a week in I realised I had a potential private buyer interested. I asked the VeloVault staff to take down the listing so I could pursue that instead. Turned out there were already three people actively bidding. It caused a lot of confusion. I think the system should make it clear when you can and can't pull a listing — you can't just yank it once people are bidding on it. I felt terrible about it, honestly. One of the bidders sent me quite an angry email. I simply didn't know people were already bidding. If I'd seen that, I would never have asked.

**Interviewer:** And if you eventually do sell the car and want to remove it from the catalog entirely?

**Diane:** That should be straightforward. But I suppose if there's still an auction running for it, or one that's about to start, there should be some kind of warning. You shouldn't be able to just delete a car that's in the middle of an auction. And please, if the answer is no, tell me why in normal language. Not "Error 409" or whatever it is. I'm not an engineer.

## Interview 3 — Tom Verhoeven, Enthusiast Buyer

| | |
|---|---|
| **Role** | Collector and buyer |
| **Age** | 47 |
| **Interview context** | Came in on his day off. Enthusiastic, talks fast, compares everything to other platforms he uses. |

**Interviewer:** Tom, how long have you been buying through VeloVault?

**Tom:** About four years. I've bought five cars. Mostly French — I have a thing for Peugeots and Citroëns from the sixties and seventies.

**Interviewer:** When and where do you usually look for cars?

**Tom:** I'm a GP in Breda, so my days are pretty full. It's usually in the evening on my phone, on the sofa, once the house is quiet. With two teenagers that can be late. [laughs] Or in the ten minutes between two patients. I do practically everything on my phone: banking, booking holidays, and I'm on two or three other auction sites as well, for parts and the occasional watch. I rarely sit down at a computer for this. If I find something I'm serious about, I'll look at it again on my laptop so I can read everything properly.

**Interviewer:** What do you look for when you're browsing?

**Tom:** I usually start by filtering on make. There's no point me scrolling through fifty German cars if I'm only interested in French ones. Sometimes I filter on year range as well — I'm not interested in anything after 1980, personally. And condition matters. I'm not a restorer, so I tend to look for cars that are in good or excellent condition.

What drives me mad on some sites is that the filters reset every time I go back to the list. I select French, before 1980, good or excellent, open one car, go back, and it's all gone. I don't have time for that between patients.

**Interviewer:** Once you find a car you like, what do you do?

**Tom:** I look at the auction. I want to know when it ends, what the current bid is, and how many other people have bid. That tells me how competitive it's going to be. If there are already eight bids and two hours left, that's a different situation than no bids and five days left. And the end time has to be crystal clear. A date and time is fine, but when it gets close I want to see how much time is left. I once missed an auction because I assumed it ended in the evening and it closed at noon.

**Interviewer:** And when you're ready to bid?

**Tom:** I put in my amount and submit. The one thing I'd expect the system to handle is making sure my bid is actually higher than what's already there. Last year on a different platform I accidentally submitted the same amount as the current bid and it just accepted it, which created a whole mess afterwards. So validation matters.

And after I submit, I want to see straight away that it worked. My amount, the time, and that I'm now the highest bidder. With the newsletter I'd email a bid and then just hope. Honestly, my heart rate goes up in the last hour of an auction. It's a bit of an adrenaline thing. It would also be nice to know when someone outbids me, but I can imagine that's for later.

**Interviewer:** Have you ever tried to bid on something and been unable to?

**Tom:** Once, yes. I came back to an auction I'd been watching and tried to bid but nothing happened. Turned out the auction had already ended. The page just hadn't refreshed. I think the system should make it obvious when an auction is no longer open — and it definitely shouldn't accept bids for something that's ended or hasn't started yet.

Oh, and one more thing about that: I'm colour-blind, red-green, which is quite common in men. Some sites show an open auction in green and a closed one in red, and to me those look practically identical. Just write it out. Open, closed, starts on Friday. Words work.

**Interviewer:** Anything else you'd want from the platform?

**Tom:** I like to see the full bid history on an auction. Not necessarily who bid — that can stay anonymous — but the amounts and when they were placed. It helps me understand how other buyers are valuing the car. And sometimes I want to come back to an auction I lost and see what it eventually went for. That history should stay accessible even after the auction ends.

**Interviewer:** Why does the anonymity matter to you?

**Tom:** The community is small. If people know I'm after a Citroën SM, the price goes up. [laughs] And looking back at auctions I lost, partly that's to learn what things are worth. Partly it stings a bit, losing a car you really wanted. Seeing what it went for tells me whether I was being sensible or just stingy.

**Interviewer:** If you could change one thing about buying through VeloVault, what would it be?

**Tom:** Keep it as trustworthy as it is now, but make it as fast as the big platforms. Right now VeloVault is personal but slow. The big platforms are fast but feel like a flea market.

## Interview 4 — Sandra Meijer, Operations Manager

| | |
|---|---|
| **Role** | Operations Manager |
| **Age** | 38 |
| **Interview context** | At her desk, with the auction spreadsheet open on one of her two monitors. The phone rang twice during the interview; she took one call. Direct and practical. |

**Interviewer:** Sandra, what does your day-to-day involvement with the platform look like?

**Sandra:** I'm the person who fields all the complaints when something goes wrong. So I need to be able to see everything — all the cars, all the auctions, all the users. And in some cases I need to be able to intervene.

**Interviewer:** Can you walk me through a typical morning?

**Sandra:** I've been here seven years. I started as an administrative assistant, answering the phone, and these days everything that isn't a car or a server ends up on my desk: onboarding sellers, buyer questions, complaints. I start at half past eight with email and voicemail. On a normal Monday that's fifteen to twenty messages. Then I update the spreadsheet. Then the phone starts. I'm usually doing three things at once, so whatever I use has to let me find things quickly. Search on a name, an email address, a car. If I have to click through five screens while someone is waiting on the phone, that's a problem.

**Interviewer:** What are those messages usually about?

**Sandra:** The same handful of things, over and over. Did you get my bid? When does it end? What's a reserve price? Why can't I bid anymore? And password problems. Always password problems. If the new platform answers those questions on the screen, I'll be a very happy woman.

**Interviewer:** You said earlier that you sometimes need to intervene. What kind of interventions?

**Sandra:** Changing the status of an auction, mostly. Sometimes a seller contacts us because they set the wrong end date. Sometimes an auction needs to be cancelled because the car was in an accident before the auction ended. I need to be able to move an auction from active back to pending, or cancel it entirely, depending on the situation.

And when I do something like cancelling, I want the system to ask me whether I'm sure, and show me exactly what I'm about to cancel. Once, in the spreadsheet, I closed the wrong auction because two cars were on rows next to each other. A Porsche 911 and another Porsche 911. That took a week of apologies.

**Interviewer:** Are there rules around those status changes?

**Sandra:** There have to be. You can't reopen an auction that's already ended, for example — that would be chaos. And you shouldn't be able to move straight from pending to ended without it ever being active. The transitions have to follow some kind of logic.

**Interviewer:** What about user management?

**Sandra:** Right now — in the old process — we onboard every seller manually. With the new platform I'd want sellers to be able to register themselves, but I'd want to be able to see a list of all registered users and, if necessary, know what role they have. If someone is abusing the platform, I need to be able to identify them. Ideally a list I can sort and filter, like a spreadsheet. When they registered, what role they have, how many cars or bids. I'm not technical, I couldn't tell you what happens behind the screen, but give me Excel and email and I can do anything. I live in Excel, so the closer it feels to that, the better.

**Interviewer:** And from a data perspective, what worries you most?

**Sandra:** Orphaned data. If a seller deletes their car but there are live auctions attached to it, those auctions become meaningless — buyers can't see the car they were bidding on. That should never be allowed to happen. The system needs to check before it deletes anything.

**Interviewer:** How quickly does that check need to happen?

**Sandra:** I'd say within a few seconds. We're not talking about batch processing. If I hit delete and wait ten seconds with no response, I assume something is broken. A couple of seconds at most, and then either the deletion goes through or I get a clear message explaining why it didn't. A message I could read out to a seller on the phone. "This car can't be removed because it has an auction that ends on the 14th." Something like that.

**Interviewer:** One last question. Is there anything you'd like to be involved in while the platform is being built?

**Sandra:** I'd like to try it before it goes live. Properly, myself, not a demo where someone clicks through it for me. And I can find you people. We hold a viewing day in the showroom on the first Saturday of every month. There are always thirty or forty regulars walking around with a coffee, buyers and sellers. Plenty of them would happily give their opinion. Most of them have an opinion about everything.

## Interview 5 — Erik Smits, Head of IT

| | |
|---|---|
| **Role** | Head of IT |
| **Age** | 41 |
| **Interview context** | Meeting room, laptop open on an architecture diagram. Precise, prefers concrete questions. |

**Interviewer:** Erik, can you give us a sense of the technical constraints the platform has to work within?

**Erik:** Sure. The first thing I want to say is that this is a multi-service architecture. We have an authentication service, a catalog service, an auction service, and a gateway in front of them. That was a deliberate decision — it means we can scale and deploy each piece independently. But it also means the services have to communicate reliably.

**Interviewer:** Before we go further: do you use the platform yourself?

**Erik:** Not as a user, no. I joined VeloVault two years ago from a logistics company, so my background is systems that move things around, not screens people look at. I look at logs and monitoring. I've placed maybe two test bids. The first version was built by an external development team, and the front end is mostly the contractors' territory. I care about what happens behind it.

**Interviewer:** Back to the services, then. How are they communicating right now?

**Erik:** The gateway talks to the other services over HTTP. That's fine for most things. But there's one case — when a car is deleted — where the catalog service needs to ask the auction service whether there are any active auctions before it goes ahead. We can't do that with a simple HTTP call and forget about it, because if the auction service is temporarily slow or restarting, we'd either block forever or make a wrong decision. So we're using an internal message broker for that specific flow.

**Interviewer:** What are the failure modes you're most concerned about?

**Erik:** Two things. First, the deletion flow I just described — what happens if the auction service doesn't respond in time? We've decided that if there's no answer within five seconds, the catalog service should go ahead and delete the car anyway, log a warning, and we'll deal with the edge case manually. We can't have a service outage block users from managing their own data indefinitely.

Second, and more broadly: if one service goes down, the others should keep running. A buyer shouldn't get a broken page just because the auction service is being redeployed.

**Interviewer:** What should that buyer see instead?

**Erik:** From my side, the gateway returns an error code when a service is unavailable. What the page does with that is up to whoever builds the front end. The first version mostly showed a blank screen.

**Interviewer:** And in the deletion flow, a user could wait up to five seconds after pressing delete?

**Erik:** Yes. For a machine, five seconds is an eternity. For a person... [shrugs] you'd have to ask them.

**Interviewer:** What about security?

**Erik:** All requests from the outside go through the gateway, which validates the token before forwarding anything. Tokens are signed JWTs — they expire after seven days. The downstream services trust the gateway, so they read the user identity from headers rather than re-validating the token themselves.

**Interviewer:** What happens when a token expires?

**Erik:** The gateway rejects the request and the user has to log in again. When that happens to the user, on which screen, in the middle of what: the backend has no idea. It just says no.

**Interviewer:** Any concerns about the current setup you'd flag for the next sprint?

**Erik:** A few. The databases are SQLite, which is fine for a prototype but not for production under real load. That migration needs to happen before we go live. Also, the message broker — Redis — gives us at-least-once delivery. That means a message could theoretically be processed twice if a service crashes at exactly the wrong moment. The handlers need to be written with that in mind — processing the same event twice shouldn't cause double-cancellations or other inconsistencies.

One smaller thing: all timestamps are stored in UTC. An auction ending at eight in the evening Amsterdam time is stored as six or seven, depending on the season. The first version showed the stored time directly, and we had a seller convinced his auction had closed an hour early.

**Interviewer:** And in terms of availability targets?

**Erik:** We haven't formalised an SLA yet, but Marcus has made it clear that auction-ending events are critical. An auction ending at two in the morning with no staff around has to work. I'd say we're aiming for the platform to be available at least ninety-nine percent of the time, with no planned downtime during active auctions.
